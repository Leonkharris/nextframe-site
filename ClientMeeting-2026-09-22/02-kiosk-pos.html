The procedure

Register, play, redeem

Walked through end to end and accepted by the client on the call. This is the design of record until someone writes a better one.

1
Register & deposit

At the POS or kiosk, using the player's phone number. A one-time password is sent and confirmed, the deposit is taken, and an account-and-password ticket is printed.

In store
2
First login

The player takes the ticket to a computer or their own phone, types the account and password, changes the password, and starts playing.

Two design arguments

Why the phone number, and why no bet tickets

1

The phone number prevents a specific argument at the counter

The reasoning is operational rather than technical: "If they play a one-time password account in the website or in the phone, and somehow they turn off the browser, they will have issue with the people in the store — because he couldn't get his one-time password back, or he lost his ticket, and it will cause chaos."

A phone number is recoverable. A printed slip is not. "Just type your phone number and you get your password. That will be easier."

The second benefit is commercial, and was stated openly: "You can trace those phone numbers and give them more marketing purposes in the future." The document lists it as a benefit too.
2

Printing a ticket for every bet was ruled out

The client's original ask was a scanner that verifies bets. The advice was to keep tickets at the money boundary only: "We would not suggest to have to bet and print out ticket, because that is complicated. It will be easier to just register and deposit in the kiosk or the POS, and get their balance."

So the ticket exists at register and at redeem, and never in between. Play happens on a screen, on a store computer or the player's own phone.

💡

One point raised and settled in the client's favour. Asked whether a cash-out should empty the account, the answer was to pay out in full — but the counter-argument was accepted immediately: leaving the account live, with the phone number as the identifier, is what lets the player keep going at home. The design above reflects that.

The recommendation

Don't buy the kiosks

The platform side worked out what the client actually needed and then told him he did not need the thing he had asked for. Worth noticing, because it costs them a hardware sale.

Kiosk

What was asked for

  • About $3,000 per machine
  • Has to integrate with the financial system or it is a separate silo
  • Described as "a hard process"
  • Acts as an automated cashier: takes deposits

POS

What was recommended

  • Just a cashier system — software, no hardware spend
  • Uses staff the game rooms already employ
  • Same register, deposit, print and redeem flow
  • Can be stood up first, with kiosks added later if wanted
🧾

The client's own argument closed it. "If there is employee, the cashier already in your game room, so you can use only the POS system. You don't need a kiosk." — answered with "Most of the places have employees working, serving coffee… so that's all employees."

He also made the comparison himself, to a casino floor: you can place a bet at the machine, but you take the ticket to a cashier to be paid. The kiosk never handles the payout anyway.

⚠️

The one caveat attached to kiosks, if they are ever revisited: "If you want to invest for this kind of machine, you need to make sure that all the system can connect with our financial system. Otherwise it will be separate." A kiosk that does not reconcile into the platform's financials is a second set of books.
