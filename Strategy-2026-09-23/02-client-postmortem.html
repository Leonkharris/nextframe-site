The diagnosis

We explained "kiosk". He needed "casino management".

The client spent a long stretch of the previous meeting asking why he needed a kiosk. The answer, in hindsight, was that nobody translated the idea into something he already knew.

0:01:30—
“Honestly, in this circle, you only know what a kiosk actually is if you're in it.”
0:01:46—
“Fundamentally this thing is called casino management. Any casino — the one downstairs at the Grand Hotel — doesn't it have a machine? You put your card in, you see how much you've played, you see your player status, whether there's any promotion.”
0:02:21—
“Whether it was explained his way or our way — he simply didn't follow it. He didn't know what we were talking about. He's in the US, the US has casinos. If you'd just said 'it's like the casino management inside a casino', he'd have understood immediately.”
0:02:43—
“We often don't strike at the other side's pain point — so they understand even less why they'd want a kiosk.”The generalisable lesson.
🔤

There is a vocabulary trap underneath this. In the US, "kiosk" usually means a self-service terminal of the commercial sort, not a casino cash machine. So the word did not merely fail to land — it actively pointed the client at the wrong mental picture, which is why he kept asking what it was for.

The practical fix is small: when introducing a component, lead with the nearest thing the client already operates, then name it. "It's the cash machine on a casino floor — we call it a kiosk."

The idea that got lost

If he doesn't want tickets, use QR

The client's objection on the day was that he did not see why bets should produce printed tickets. That objection was accepted — but a solution to it existed and was only raised afterwards, here.

📱

"Dats said one thing — he said, I'm playing on his, why would I still print out tickets? Fine, we don't print out tickets. Can we do a casino network? Actually we still can. Just use a QR code, right?"

That is a direct answer to the client's stated objection, and it was not offered while he was in the room. Worth putting on the agenda for the architecture meeting that the client call agreed to hold.

How the account actually runs

The support model, described from the inside

Useful context for anyone picking this client up, because it is not written down anywhere else.

The cadence
Request → assess → ship weekly → train

"Our weekly meeting is basically aimed at the requirements he raises. We assess them, decide what's worth changing, ship the changes each week, then tell him what the new feature does and teach him how to operate it." If nothing changed that week, the call is used to check how he is getting on.

The channel
A direct group chat, every day

The client is in a group with the platform team and raises problems daily. That arrangement was not the original plan — it emerged because the intermediary could not resolve the issues himself.

The friction
Opposite time zones

Named as a structural constraint rather than a complaint: "we need to sleep, and his hours are the reverse of ours." Same issue that delayed the asset handoff in the 30 September meeting.

The structure
The client is one line among several

The intermediary holds a master agency and has given out other lines besides this one — "and he doesn't know that." Relevant to how much weight his requests should carry against other lines.

🎯

One observation worth keeping separate from the rest. The people positioned as the operators of this account were described as not knowing how to operate it, with the client himself effectively doing the operating. If that is right, it changes who training and documentation should be aimed at — and it is testable rather than a matter of opinion: look at who actually files the requests and who logs into the back office.
