Getting AI to Write Real Taglish, Not Textbook Tagalog

By Leon Harris, Founder & AI Creative Director · Updated September 2026

You ask ChatGPT for a "casual, friendly caption" and what comes back reads like a government memo: deep Tagalog no customer of yours actually types, formal words in the wrong places, po dropped mid-sentence. So you give up, post in English only, and the customers who would have commented, messaged and booked in Taglish just scroll past. The fix is not a cleverer instruction — it is showing the AI what your customers already sound like, and this article walks you through exactly how.

Why AI defaults to stiff Tagalog

AI writing tools learn from the text that is abundant and clean online: news articles, government and legal documents, encyclopedia entries, translated material. The mixed-language way Filipinos actually write Facebook captions and Messenger replies is far scarcer as raw material, and much of it sits inside private accounts and closed groups. No AI company publishes exactly what went into its training, so treat that as an explanation rather than proven fact — but it matches what you see. Ask for "casual" and the tool falls back to the safest, most formal Filipino it knows.

There is an official angle too. The Komisyon sa Wikang Filipino has publicly discouraged Taglish — its chairman said in 2022 that mixing English and Filipino damages the structure of both. The textbook Tagalog your AI keeps producing is closer to what a language-purist government body prescribes than to what your customers type in your comments section. That is the "proper" version the tool reaches for when you do not steer it somewhere else.

The one fix that actually works

Adjectives do not work. "Make it casual," "make it friendly," "sound natural" — none of these tell the tool how formal you actually want it, so it reverts to formal. What works is pasting 5 to 10 of your own past captions, or real customer Messenger threads and comments, straight into the chat, then writing: "Match the tone, sentence length and English-Filipino mix of these examples exactly. Do not write more formally than these." Concrete examples steer a writing tool far more reliably than any description of a style. This costs nothing extra — the free tiers of ChatGPT, Google Gemini and Claude all accept pasted examples, within their daily usage limits.

Prompt moves worth making

  • Paste real examples first — nothing below matters as much as this one.
  • Name the city or region — "Cebu customers" or "Quezon City audience" shifts the vocabulary on its own.
  • Say who is reading — repeat customers who already trust you need a different tone than cold traffic from an ad.
  • Ask for three versions at different mixes — mostly English, balanced Taglish, deeper Filipino — so you pick instead of re-prompting from scratch.
  • Ban formal words as you catch them — if "angkop," "mapagkukunan" or "inyong" keep appearing, keep a running "do not use" list in your prompt.
  • Keep English for terms nobody translates — book, delivery, cash on delivery, downpayment, unit, viewing, deposit, sched. Forcing these into formal Filipino is the clearest stiffness signal there is.

Which language a post should even be in

This is an audience decision, not a matter of taste. Straight English tends to fit corporate or expat-facing services, where formality signals professionalism. Taglish fits most consumer-facing local businesses — food, retail, beauty, agents selling to local buyers — because that is literally how those customers comment and message you. Deeper, less English-mixed Filipino fits community and provincial audiences, especially older readers, where it also reads as respect. You do not have to guess: open your inbox, read the last twenty customer messages, and match those.

What to check before you post

Read every draft for five failure signs. Business terms over-translated into formal Filipino no customer would use, like "paghahatid" for delivery. Po and opo used inconsistently or dropped mid-sentence. A formal clause sitting beside a casual one in the same caption, which reads like two different writers. Invented idioms and proverb-sounding lines that are not real Filipino sayings — these tools do produce those. And vocabulary from the wrong region, like Manila slang aimed at a Cebuano-speaking audience. One more test sits outside the draft: if you or your staff cannot hold the voice the AI wrote, do not post it. The mismatch shows the moment you reply to a comment in your own words, in the same thread.

The honest limits

These tools are noticeably weaker outside Tagalog and English, and the companies themselves show it. Anthropic's published multilingual results table for Claude covers fourteen languages and includes no Filipino or Tagalog at all. Google has announced Project SEALD with AI Singapore, work aimed at improving AI for Southeast Asian languages including Tagalog, Cebuano and Ilocano — the fact that this is still ongoing work tells you these languages are not solved.

That matters more than it sounds. Per the 2020 census, Tagalog is the language spoken at home in 39.9% of Philippine households, while Bisaya/Binisaya accounts for 16.0%, Hiligaynon 7.3%, Ilocano 7.1% and Cebuano a further 6.5%. If your buyers are outside NCR and Southern Tagalog, "the AI writes fine Filipino" tells you nothing about whether it writes fine Ilonggo. For those languages, have a native speaker — staff, family, whoever actually talks to that audience — read the draft before it goes live. That step is not optional.

Frequently asked questions

Bakit parang deep Tagalog o government memo ang lumalabas kahit sinabi kong "gawing casual"?

Words like "casual" or "friendly" do not actually tell the tool how formal you want it, so it falls back to the formal Filipino it saw most. The reliable fix is pasting 5 to 10 of your own real captions or customer messages and telling it to match those exactly. Real examples do more than any adjective.

Taglish ba talaga dapat lahat ng post ng small business ko?

Not always. It is an audience decision. Taglish fits most consumer-facing local businesses (food, retail, beauty, local real estate) because that is how those customers comment and message. Straight English tends to fit corporate or expat-facing services. Deeper, less English-mixed Filipino tends to fit community and provincial audiences, especially older readers. Match whatever your own customers already use in your inbox.

Ligtas bang gamitin ang AI para sa Bisaya, Ilocano o Hiligaynon na caption?

Be more careful here. Anthropic's own published multilingual table for Claude lists fourteen languages and none of them are Philippine languages, and Google frames its Southeast Asian language work (Project SEALD, with AI Singapore) as still ongoing. Treat AI output in these languages as a rough draft and have a native speaker read it before you post.

Paano ko pipigilan ang AI sa pag-translate ng "delivery" o "downpayment"?

Put it in the prompt directly: keep these terms in English because that is how customers actually use them, then name them — book, delivery, cash on delivery, downpayment, unit, viewing, deposit, reservation, sched. Over-translating these into formal Filipino is the single clearest sign of textbook output.

Ilang example caption ba ang kailangan ko?

Start with 5 to 10 and lean toward the higher end if your posts vary a lot by type (promo, announcement, reply). Choose ones that genuinely sound like you rather than your best-performing ones. Past ten or so, extra examples add little and just make the prompt long.

Kailangan ko pa bang basahin isa-isa kahit may examples na ako binigay?

Yes. Check each draft for over-translated business terms, inconsistent po and opo, a formal sentence sitting next to a casual one, invented Filipino sayings, and vocabulary from the wrong region. The review also protects your voice — if the caption is written in a register you cannot sustain when replying to comments live, customers will notice the gap.
